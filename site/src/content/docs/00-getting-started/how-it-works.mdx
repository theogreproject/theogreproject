If “course” makes you picture homework, grades, and someone yelling “YOU’RE LATE,” breathe. That’s not what we’re doing here.

The Ogre Project is built like a workshop you can walk through at your own pace. You read a small thing, you try a small thing, you keep what works, and you move on. The whole design is meant to make you feel like you’re _actually getting better_, not like you’re collecting tips you’ll forget the moment the session starts.

This page is here to show you the engine under the hood. Once you understand the loop, you’ll always know what you’re doing, why you’re doing it, and what “progress” looks like in practical terms.

<Aside type="tip">
  If you ever feel lost, come back to this page. The answer is almost always: “do the next small
  rep.”
</Aside>

## The Ogre learning loop

Most lessons in this course follow the same rhythm. Not because we’re obsessed with structure, but because GMing is a craft, and crafts improve through feedback loops.

Here’s the loop you’ll see again and again:

{/* prettier-ignore */}
<Steps>
  1. **Learn** — you get one principle and one procedure. Not a speech. Something you can actually use.
  2. **Drill** — you practice a single skill in a short, focused way (usually 5–20 minutes).
  3. **Build** — you turn that skill into an **artifact**: a small piece of material you can reuse at the table.
  4. **Playtest** — you try it. With a group, with one person, or even with a solo simulation.
  5. **Debrief** — you look at what happened: what worked, what didn’t, what surprised you.
  6. **Iterate** — you make one small change and keep the version that behaves better at the table.
</Steps>

That’s it. That’s the whole philosophy.

Sometimes you’ll do the entire loop in one sitting. Sometimes you’ll spread it across a week. Sometimes your “playtest” is a real session, and sometimes it’s a tiny test run of one scene. The point is consistency: learn a thing, try a thing, improve a thing.

<Aside type="note">
  You don’t need to “finish the course” to benefit from it. Every time you complete a loop, you
  become more reliable at the table.
</Aside>

## What is a drill, really?

A drill is practice that is deliberately small. It’s not “write a campaign.” It’s more like: “write three clearer hooks,” or “turn one vague room into a room that prompts action,” or “take one NPC and give them a usable goal and a trigger.”

Drills are short on purpose. When practice is small, you actually do it. When practice is huge, you postpone it forever, and then you feel guilty, and then the hobby becomes a job. We don’t want that.

A good drill has two qualities:

First, it targets **one skill**. Not five. Not “make everything better.” One.

Second, it produces something you can look at and say, “yeah, that changed.” Even if the change is tiny.

## What is an artifact?

An artifact is something you keep.

It can be a page, a checklist, a script, a table, a map, a one-shot outline, a faction clock, a set of rumors, a monster control card -- anything that makes your next session easier and better.

Artifacts are how this course becomes a portfolio. Not in a “job interview” way (though that can happen). In a very practical way: you end up with a toolbox you can reuse instead of reinventing everything every week.

And yes: artifacts can be messy at first. You’re not publishing on day one. You’re building version 1, testing it, and improving it.

## What each lesson will usually contain

Most lessons are written in a consistent format so you always know what to expect.

You’ll usually see:

- a short **Introduction** that frames the skill,
- a **Lesson**, divided into sections, that explains the principle and the procedure in plain language,
- an **Assignment** that includes a drill and/or an artifact (sometimes very light, especially in onboarding),
- a **Knowledge check** with a few questions that help you notice whether you understood the idea,
- and **Additional Resources** if you want to go deeper.

Some lessons also include “clinics.” Clinics are little focused help for problems that show up in real games, like decision paralysis, pacing in dungeons, or what to do when a scene goes sideways. They’re the “this happened to me last Saturday, help” parts of the course.

## How to move through the course

The course is organized into tracks, and the tracks have a recommended order. You can wander if you want, after all it’s a hobby, but the recommended sequence exists because it makes later skills easier.

The short version is: we build the human foundation first, then the player foundation (because great GMs come from great players), then the table foundation, then the session craft, then challenges, then campaigns, then worldbuilding, then publication.

## How much time do you need?

Less than your anxiety thinks.

If you have 30 minutes in a week, you can read one lesson and do one small drill. That’s already progress. If you have more time, you can do bigger reps, but bigger reps are optional. The only thing that really matters is that you keep returning.

If you ever feel like you’re “not doing enough,” remember: your real practice happens when you’re making decisions under uncertainty. That’s GMing. The course is just giving you better defaults and a better feedback loop.

<Aside type="tip">
  The healthiest mindset here is: “I’m building a habit.” Not: “I must catch up.”
</Aside>

## Assignment

For this page, keep it light. Your job is to understand the loop and know where you’re going next.

If you want a tiny warm-up, do this in your head (no writing required): when you think about your next game, what feels more useful right now — learning a new idea, or improving one thing you already do?

## Knowledge check

Answer in one or two sentences each.

- What’s the difference between a **drill** and an **artifact**?
- In the Ogre loop, what comes right after **playtest**?
- If you miss a week, what should you do next?
- What would be a “tiny rep” you could realistically do in 15 minutes?
