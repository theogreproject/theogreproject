Before we talk about tools, let’s do the most important thing: lower your shoulders.

You do **not** need a perfect setup to become a better GM. You don’t need a fancy VTT. You don’t need a “worldbuilding app.” You don’t need to buy a stack of books. You don’t need special dice, a special notebook, or a sacred ritual under the full moon.

(Altough if you want those things, that’s cool too. And, experiencing a real full moon ritual might just be that thing you need for your next adventure hook.)

What you need is a way to capture ideas, a way to keep your work organized, and a way to come back next week and keep going. That’s it.

This page is intentionally boring in the best way. It’s here to remove friction, not add it.

## The absolute minimum

If you have these three things, you’re good:

First, you need **a place to write**. It can be a notebook, a notes app, a text file, or a doc in the cloud. The only requirement is that it’s easy to open and you don’t feel “blocked” by it.

Second, you need **a place to store artifacts**. This course produces small, reusable materials. If you keep them scattered across random apps and screenshots, you’ll lose them. A single folder is enough. Call it “Ogre” and move on with your life.

Third, you need **a way to playtest**. Ideally that’s your real table, but it can also be a one-on-one, a short Discord call, or solo simulation. We’ll teach you how to test without a perfect group.

That’s the minimum. Everything else is optional.

<Aside type="note">
  You can do this course with nothing but a phone and a notes app. It’s not ideal, but it works.
</Aside>

## The Ogre Journal (your practice log)

If there’s one thing I’ll gently recommend, it’s having an **Ogre Journal**.

Not because journaling is magical, but because it gives you a simple feedback loop. After you run something (or even after you read a lesson), you capture what happened in a few lines. That’s how you turn experience into skill.

Your journal can be one file, one doc, or a folder of tiny notes. It doesn’t need to look pretty. The only goal is: when you come back later, you can see your own progress and notice patterns.

A good journal entry is short. It’s usually just: what you tried, what happened, what you’ll change next time.

## A small toolbox

You don’t need any of the tools below to succeed in this course. This section exists for one reason: so you can look at the hobby and think, “oh, right! Options.”

Also: tools change. Prices change. Features move around. That’s fine. Think of this as a friendly starting list, not a sacred canon.

We’ll keep updating this page as the course grows and we reference new tools in future lessons.

### Writing & notes

If you want your notes to be easy to find later, pick one “home” and stick to it for a while.

- [Obsidian](https://obsidian.md/) — simple, fast markdown notes, great for personal wikis.
- [Notion](https://www.notion.com/) — docs and databases in one place; great if you like organizing things visually.
- [Google Docs](https://docs.google.com/) — the classic: simple, shareable, and hard to break.
- A plain folder of `.doc` files — genuinely underrated. Boring. Reliable. Ogre-approved.

### Campaign & world organization

These help when you want your NPCs, locations, and plots to stop living in your head.

- [Kanka](https://kanka.io/) — campaign manager with a free tier; great for organizing people/places/factions.
- [World Anvil](https://www.worldanvil.com/) — worldbuilding platform with a free tier; good if you like structured articles and lore pages.

### Maps (battle, dungeon, towns, regions)

Maps are optional, but clarity is not. If maps help your table, here are some easy starters:

- [Dungeon Scrawl](https://www.dungeonscrawl.com/) — fast dungeon maps in the browser (great for “I need a map in 10 minutes”).
- [Inkarnate](https://inkarnate.com/) — beautiful maps with a free plan (limited, but enough to learn and experiment).
- [Watabou’s City Generator](https://watabou.itch.io/medieval-fantasy-city-generator) — instant town/city layouts you can screenshot and annotate.

### VTTs (virtual tabletops)

If you play online, you’ll probably want one of these. If you play in person, you can ignore this completely.

- [Roll20](https://roll20.net/) — big, popular, feature-rich, usable for free.
- [Owlbear Rodeo](https://www.owlbear.rodeo/) — lightweight and fast; great when you just want a map and tokens without a ton of setup.

<Aside type="tip">
  If you’re new to VTTs, start with the simplest tool that solves your problem. You can always
  “graduate” later.
</Aside>

### Dice & online table tools

If you play online (or even hybrid), these can make life smoother.

- [Discord](https://discord.com/) — text, voice, video, and channels; the default “table hub” for many groups.
- [Avrae](https://avrae.io/) — a powerful Discord dice bot (especially handy if your group likes automation).
- [RollAdvantage Dice Roller](https://rolladvantage.com/diceroller/) — quick browser dice when you don’t want a bot.

### Tokens, handouts, and visuals

Sometimes one little image does more than five paragraphs.

- [Token Stamp 2](https://rolladvantage.com/tokenstamp/) — turn any image into a clean VTT token in seconds.
- [Canva](https://www.canva.com/) — easy handouts, quick cards, “fake book pages,” signs, posters, session recaps.
- [Excalidraw](https://excalidraw.com/) — simple whiteboard-style drawings; great for quick diagrams and flowcharts.

### Music & ambience (optional spice)

This is not required, but it can be fun. Keep it simple.

- [Tabletop Audio](https://tabletopaudio.com/) — great ambient tracks for RPG sessions.
- [Syrinscape](https://syrinscape.com/) — very powerful soundscapes (freemium-ish; you can try it and see if you like the vibe).

## Books and references

You don’t need to read a library to do this course. When we point you to references, it will be because they’re genuinely useful, not because they’re “required reading.”

If you like reading RPG theory, great — you’ll have optional rabbit holes. If you don’t, you can still progress through the entire course using only what’s on the site.

## A note about “system-agnostic”

This course aims to teach skills that travel well across systems. That means you don’t need a specific ruleset to start.

But you do need one tiny thing: the willingness to translate procedures into your table’s reality. If your system has no initiative, you’ll adapt the combat pacing tools. If your system has no maps, you’ll adapt the spatial clarity tools. If your system is narrative-first, you’ll still use the same ideas: clarity, choices, consequences, and momentum.

The course will help you do that translation without making it feel like homework.

<Aside type="tip">
  If you’re currently playing one system, stick with it while you learn. Switching systems
  mid-learning can blur feedback.
</Aside>

## Assignment

Nothing to buy. Nothing to install.

You can browse the list of optional tools above if you want ideas, but it’s not required.

If you want to set yourself up gently, do one simple thing: decide where your artifacts will live. Create a folder or a doc. Name it. Done.
