“Playtest” can sound scary. Like you need a full group, a perfect session, and a clipboard.

You don’t.

In this course, playtesting is just a fancy way to say: **try the thing once and see what happens**. Not forever. Not perfectly. Once.

Most GM problems aren’t solved by reading more advice. They’re solved by running one small experiment, noticing what changed, and keeping the version that behaves better at the table.

That’s what we mean by playtesting. And yes — you can do it even if you don’t have a stable group right now.

<Aside type="tip">If you can test one idea in five minutes, you can improve forever.</Aside>

## Lesson

### What playtesting is (and what it isn’t)

Playtesting is not an exam. It’s not “prove you’re good.” It’s not “run a perfect one-shot.”

Playtesting is simply giving your work contact with reality.

You made a rumor table? Try using it at the start of a scene. You wrote a clearer hook? Say it out loud. You made an NPC with a goal and trigger? Put them in front of the players and see what they do. You created a fight with a cool gimmick? Run two rounds and watch how it feels.

A test can be tiny. The value is that it produces evidence. And evidence is what turns “I think this will work” into “this actually worked.”

### The smallest possible playtest

When in doubt, make the test smaller.

Instead of testing a whole session, test:

- one scene opener,
- one choice point,
- one clue drop,
- one NPC interaction,
- one “when the players do X, I do Y” rule,
- one encounter gimmick for three rounds.

The smallest possible playtest is the one you will actually do.

<Aside type="note">
  Your goal is not “finish content.” Your goal is “improve the next decision you’ll make at the
  table.”
</Aside>

### Three ways to playtest (group, one-on-one, solo)

There are a few common situations, and all of them are valid.

#### 1) Playtest at your real table

This is the easiest version, because you’re already playing. You don’t need to announce “THIS IS A PLAYTEST.” You just quietly test one thing.

You can treat the session like a normal session, and simply choose one small focus. For example: “Tonight I’m testing a clearer scene opener,” or “Tonight I’m testing whether my clues are landing.”

After the session, you do a tiny debrief, adjust one thing, and you’re done. That’s a full loop.

#### 2) Playtest with one person (or two)

If you don’t have a group, a single player is enough to test a lot of skills. In fact, one-on-one is amazing for practice because it’s fast.

You can do a 15–30 minute micro-session: one scene, one decision, one consequence.

This is also a great way to test clarity. If your scene opener is confusing, one person will show you immediately. If your choice is fake, one person will poke the hole immediately. That’s good. That’s the point.

#### 3) Solo simulation (yes, it counts)

Solo playtesting is not “writing in your head.” It’s structured simulation.

You take a small piece of content and you run it through a simple procedure:

You state what the players see. You state what the players want. You state what the opposition wants. You run a few “if/then” decisions. You roll dice if you want, or you use random tables, or you just choose plausible outcomes.

The goal is not to predict the future perfectly. The goal is to stress-test your design.

Solo simulation answers questions like:

Will the scene actually start?
Do the players have something to do?
Are there clear choices?
Do consequences exist?
Is the challenge readable, or is it a surprise punch?

If your content survives solo simulation, it’s usually much stronger at the real table.

<Aside type="tip">
  Solo playtesting is especially good for: hooks, clues, scene flow, pacing, and “what happens if
  they ignore this?”
</Aside>

### How to get feedback without making it awkward

A lot of GMs avoid feedback because they imagine it as a big emotional conversation.

It doesn’t have to be.

Good feedback is short, specific, and easy to answer. You’re not asking players to become critics. You’re asking them to help you steer the next session.

Instead of “So… what did you think?”, ask questions that point at usable information. Questions like:

What moment felt most fun?
What moment felt confusing?
Was there any point where you didn’t know what to do next?
Did anything feel too slow or too rushed?

When you ask like this, you get signal. And when you get signal, you can improve one thing.

### The one-minute debrief (the Ogre version)

Here’s the debrief that keeps the hobby light.

You don’t need a big journal entry. You need a tiny record you can actually maintain.

A one-minute debrief is basically three sentences:

What worked.
What didn’t.
What I’ll change next time.

That’s it.

If you only do that, you’ll improve faster than someone who writes a 2,000-word essay and never changes anything.

### The “one change” rule

If you try to fix everything at once, you won’t know what helped.

So here’s a friendly constraint: after a test, choose **one change**.

One change keeps your brain calm. One change keeps the craft learnable. One change keeps the hobby fun.

Over time, those one changes stack into a completely different GM.

<Aside type="note">The goal is not to become perfect. The goal is to become reliable.</Aside>

## Assignment

This assignment is meant to be gentle. You’re not building a module. You’re just proving to yourself that you can test something small.

Pick one tiny thing you’ve made so far — or, if you haven’t made anything yet, pick one tiny thing you can create in five minutes (like a hook, a rumor, or a single NPC goal).

Then choose one of the three playtest modes: group, one-on-one, or solo simulation.

<Steps>
  1. Choose a tiny thing to test (one scene opener, one hook, one NPC, one clue, or one gimmick). 2.
  Choose a test mode: real table / one-on-one / solo simulation. 3. Run the test for 5–15 minutes.
  4. Write a one-minute debrief: what worked, what didn’t, what you’ll change.
</Steps>
